What we are actually doing,

A short guide to the project for the four of us — no background assumed. It explains the idea, the six things we trained, how we judged them, what came out, and what is inside the presentation and the draft paper.

The one-sentence version. Big models learn language by hiding words and guessing them, but most of the cost is the guessing — so we asked whether guessing something else would give a better model for the same money. We tested six variations under identical conditions. None of them beat an untrained model on our downstream test, and we can say precisely why that is worth reporting.

01What a model actually learns

A language model does not store words. It turns each word into a list of numbers, and learns which numbers belong near each other.

Think of it as a very large address book. “Doctor” and “nurse” end up with similar addresses. “Doctor” and “bicycle” do not. That address — a list of 256 numbers in our case — is called a representation (also called an embedding or a latent). Everything else in the project is a way of making those addresses meaningful.

Why we cannot simply copy BERT

BERT, the model that made this approach standard, was trained on about 3.3 billion tokens. A token is roughly three-quarters of a word, so that is a library of books. We had a free GPU and time for about 5 million tokens — roughly 0.15% of BERT. At that scale nobody knows whether the usual recipe still works, which is the honest starting point of this project.

So the question is narrow, and answerable. Hold the model, the data, the budget and the evaluation fixed. Change only what the model is asked to predict. Measure what happens to the numbers it learns.

02Hiding words — and why guessing them is a weak target

The standard training game is called masked language modelling (MLM). Take a sentence, hide about 15% of the words, and ask the model to fill in the blanks. It gets a grade based on how well it guessed.

The trouble is the answer key is often not unique. “The doctor examined the patient” could equally be “wound”, “report”, “X-ray”. The model is penalised for a sensible guess, and rewarded for memorising which word happened to appear in the text. That noise is the thing we wanted to measure instead of assuming.

What we measured

We took text the model had not seen, hid one word at a time — 26,840 positions in total — and recorded how uncertain the model's guess was, in a unit called nats. More nats means “many answers are plausible”; near zero means “only one word fits”.

Uncertainty is not spread evenly. Rare words are harder to guess (1.52 nats) than common words (1.11 nats). So the blanks that carry the most meaning are exactly the ones where a word-level target teaches the least.

This is the motivation for the whole project. If a third of the training signal is “guess between five reasonable words”, then the model spends capacity on coin-flips. Can we train it to predict something steadier instead?

03The six things we trained

Same encoder, same data, same budget, same schedule. Only the target changes. Three were the original plan; three exist because a reviewer would rightly ask “did you check?”.

What “the teacher” is

For A and B we need something to compare against — an answer key. We keep a second copy of the same model and update it very slowly (99.6% old, 0.4% new at each step), so it drifts instead of jumping. It is called the EMA teacher: exponential moving average. The idea is borrowed from vision models; a slow twin gives steadier targets than a model that changes every step.

But nobody had checked whether the twin is necessary for our size, which is exactly what condition J does: delete the twin, use the student's own answer, and see if anything gets worse.

Why “collapse” matters, and what the seatbelts do

If you train a model to predict its own internal numbers, it can cheat: give every sentence the same numbers. The prediction becomes trivially perfect and the model learns nothing — this failure is called collapse. The two seatbelts (VICReg and Barlow Twins) are standard tricks that forbid that cheat by forcing the numbers to keep varying and to stay independent of each other.

04How we judged whether any of it worked

Good training loss proves nothing on its own — a model can get better at the game and still produce useless numbers. We used three independent checks.

Check 1 — Freeze the model and use it for something real

We take the trained encoder, freeze it completely, and train a very simple classifier on top of AG News headlines (AG News: world, sports, business, science). If the numbers were meaningful, four topics should be easy to separate. This is called a linear probe, and it is the standard measure of representation quality.

Check 2 — Look at the shape of the numbers (effective rank)

If a model uses 256 dimensions but the numbers only really vary along 12 of them, it is wasting most of its capacity. Effective rank counts how many directions the data actually spreads across. It catches collapse even when the loss looks healthy.

Check 3 — Measure how ambiguous the target is

The entropy study from section 02. It tests the premise of the project rather than the model.

Why three checks and not one. Loss can improve while the numbers get worse (we saw it). Rank can rise while accuracy stays flat (we saw that too). Only reporting all three keeps us honest about which claim is supported by which measurement.

Three findings, in order of how uncomfortable they are

  • Nothing beat the untrained model. Best single run 0.607 against 0.632 for the untrained encoder; the table above shows two-seed means, which peak at 0.597.
  • More training made it worse. 0.69M → 5M tokens cost every condition 0.02–0.05 accuracy while the training loss kept improving.
  • The seatbelts and the teacher are not the answer. The anti-collapse tricks helped a little at the larger budget but never reached random, and removing the teacher helped more than either — which argues against our own original design.
Our leading explanation. Training only ever shows the model damaged sentences; the probe shows it clean ones. Extra tokens make it better at damaged text and no better at clean text, because the clean path is never trained. One experiment settles it: probe the same models with holes in the input.

The three things worth remembering

1 · The question was fair

We measured that a third of masked positions have no single right answer, so asking the model to guess words is genuinely a noisy target.

2 · The answer was no

Six objectives, two budgets, two seeds, 24 runs. Not one of them beat an encoder that was never trained at all.

3 · The negative result is useful

We ruled out collapse. We ruled out the teacher. That leaves one testable explanation, and we know what experiment settles it.
